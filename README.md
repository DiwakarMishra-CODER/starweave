## New here? Start with this

**Indie** is music made outside the big record labels, usually by bands who care more about sounding like themselves than about selling records. You may know some of them: Radiohead, the Strokes, Arctic Monkeys, Phoebe Bridgers, Tame Impala.

**Influence** is when one artist hears another and it changes what they make. Radiohead's singer Thom Yorke has said that discovering R.E.M. "turned my life upside down", and R.E.M. went into the music he made. Radiohead in turn shaped younger bands like Deftones and Geese. That's a chain: R.E.M. → Radiohead → Geese.

**Starweave draws every chain like that it can prove,** for 312 artists, on one map. Pick a band you like and you can see who they learned from, who learned from them, and hear each one.

**You don't need to know any of the genre names.** Words like *shoegaze* or *post-punk* are just names for styles of music, and each one has its own page explaining it in plain words, with songs to play.

**Three things to try on the site:**
1. **Click any star** on the map. You'll see that artist's musical family: who shaped them above, who they shaped below. Press play to hear them.
2. **Press "Connect two artists"** and pick two bands you know. The map finds the chain between them, step by step, with proof for every step.
3. **Open the Mixtape** (top menu) to hear why the map exists, told as a mixtape.

## What it is

Starweave is a map of influence in indie music. Each star is an artist. Each line is one artist saying, or being reported as saying, that another artist shaped them. Follow the lines back far enough and almost all of them reach the Velvet Underground.

**Every link has a source.** Wherever a link appears (an artist's panel, their family photo, a road between two artists) it comes with the sentence that proves it and a **Source ↗** button that opens the interview, article, video or book page it came from. 772 of the 1,024 links are the artist speaking for themselves ("I grew up on…", "we were obsessed with…"). The rest are reported by a publication (224) or argued by a critic (28). Nothing on the map is a guess, and nothing is there just because two bands sound alike.

**Denials count too.** When an artist has said "no, we were never influenced by them", the denial is recorded (18 of them, each with its source) so the link is never added by mistake later. Car Seat Headrest's Will Toledo saying he grew up on the Beatles and the Kinks, not Pavement, Sonic Youth or Dinosaur Jr., is one. They show on the artist's page under their family photo.

**Every page is a room in the indie world.** The map is a night sky, an artist's page is a Tiny Desk-style room with their band set up in it, the genres page is a music paper with a pull-out poster of a city at night, the scenes page is slide film on a lightbox, the browse page is a festival bill, and the About page is a mixtape. Everything that looks playable can be played.

## Why I made this

> I was never interested in music. Then I heard Radiohead.

> I'd hear songs on streaming apps. They were fine the first time; by the fourth or fifth listen I was bored. Everything sounded the same. No artist had anything of their own, nothing I couldn't hear in someone else's music. I thought music just wasn't for me.

> I made this to show influence in music. In indie, it matters more than anything: the mainstream counts sales, indie counts influence. And influence can't always be heard.

> Look Alvvays up on a streaming app and you're shown bands that sound like them. You don't see the bands they actually learned from, and those are the bands I love.

These are from the Mixtape page, where I tell the whole story as a tape, one song per part: the song that changed it, how one band led to another, my two favourite bands, how I find new music, and how the map knows what it knows.

## How a link earns its place

This is the part the whole project depends on. Anyone can draw lines between bands; the work is deciding which lines are true.

**The bar for a link.** One artist shaped another if an artist says so: that they loved, grew up on, were obsessed with or kept playing someone while making their own music. Artists rarely use the word "influenced". The same thing reported by a journalist, a bandmate or the other artist counts too, one level down. A critic's comparison only counts when the critic is arguing that one band drew on the other.

**Each link shows how strong its proof is:**

| Level | Links | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **The artist's own words** | 772 (75%) | They said it themselves, in an interview, a video, a book or their own writing |
| **Reported** | 224 | A publication states it, without quoting them |
| **A critic's argument** | 28 | A critic argues that one drew on the other |

**And each link points to its proof.** Every link was checked against the page that carries it: the exact sentence was found on the page, the right person was speaking, the dates work, and the link points that way round. That page is the **Source ↗** button. The sources come from 242 different websites (The Quietus, Pitchfork, the Guardian, Rolling Stone, YouTube interviews and many more), plus 21 books cited by page. Each **Source ↗** opens on the sentence itself, highlighted, in browsers that support it. A page that has gone offline is linked through its Internet Archive copy.

**What doesn't count, learned the hard way:**
- **Sounding alike.** "Fans of X will love Y" is a comparison, not influence. Streaming apps already show you the soundalikes; this map shows who an artist actually learned from.
- **Working together.** A shared producer, a shared label, a band member in two bands or a guest spot looks like a link and isn't one.
- **A cover version,** even a whole album of covers. It shows love for a song, not a claim of influence.
- **A bare favourites list** with no comment.
- **Critics' dates.** One critic named five bands as descendants of a band; three of them formed before it. Every link is checked against release dates.
- **AI-written wikis and copied bios.** Grokipedia, an AI-generated wiki, is banned: it swaps in believable but invented influences. The same bio copied across ten sites is one unsourced claim, not ten.
- **Search summaries.** A search tool once made up a quote outright. A quote only goes on the map once it has been found on the page itself.

**Who gets in.** The map is the underground and art-rock tradition, starting from the Velvet Underground. An artist from outside indie joins only if they belong to that tradition (King Crimson, Boris, Steve Reich) and artists on the map say in their own words that they shaped them. That's why the Beatles aren't here, even though 25 artists on the map name them: the mainstream would turn this into a general history of rock. An artist with no proven links is left off rather than added as a lone star.

**Loud artists never play by surprise.** The Dillinger Escape Plan and Converge are marked loud: the site never plays them on shuffle, radio or a road, only when you press them yourself, at half volume, with a "Loud" tag on the button.

## How Connect two finds a road

The shortest chain between two artists often runs through the weakest proof. So each step gets a price based on how good its proof is: a step in the artist's own words is cheap (0.10), a reported one costs more (0.35), a critic's argument more again (0.60). Then a standard route-finding method (Dijkstra's algorithm, the same idea as a sat-nav finding the quickest road) picks the **cheapest** chain, not the one with the fewest steps. The best-sourced road is usually just one step longer. The road panel shows each step's direction and source, and says when two artists meet in a shared ancestor rather than descending from one another.

## Engineering notes

*For developers: how it's built, and the problems worth solving along the way.*

- **One map that never reloads.** Every page lives inside one Next.js route group whose layout holds the map, so moving between pages never rebuilds it. A page that sat outside the group used to rebuild the map on its first visit, a delay of about 5 seconds. Each page sits over the map on an opaque layer, and the map stops drawing while it's covered.
- **The layout is worked out at build time.** A force simulation places all 312 artists once during the build and saves each one's position, so the browser opens on a settled map with no shuffle. Each group is pulled toward its own fixed angle around the centre, so adding a group can't move the others.
- **The canvas redraws every frame** (the zoomed-out view brightens continuously with zoom), so every glow is drawn once into a small off-screen image and stamped, never rebuilt per star per frame, and canvas blur is banned from the draw loop. Rebuilding a glow every frame once caused severe lag.
- **Clicks are checked against where stars are drawn right now,** not the chart library's hidden hit map, which could still hold a star's old position after the family view moved it.
- **The data is built, not served from a database.** `data/seed-data.ts` holds who influenced whom; `data/edge-sources.json` holds each link's proof (the page, the exact sentence found on it, the checked wording). `scripts/build-graph.ts` merges them, checks that every link points at a real artist, fetches artist photos (Deezer), album covers (Apple Music, the Cover Art Archive) and 30-second song previews (Apple Music), lays out the map and writes `public/graph.json`. Every network call has a timeout, so a silent server can't hang the build. Around 50 photos and a dozen covers that the automatic search got wrong (often a stranger with the same name) are pinned by hand, each with the reason.
- **The genre city is laid out by algorithms, then tested.** Heavy-path decomposition keeps each genre's biggest child on its parent's road; greedy interval colouring packs unrelated genres onto shared roads, so the 52 dated genres fit on 13 rows. Time is not a straight year scale: each sign sits as far left as it can without overlapping the one before it. Sign widths come from the font's real measured letter widths, and tests check that every name fits its sign and that no pinned note covers a lit sign.
- **The scenes lightbox is laid out the same way:** scenes that don't overlap in time share a row, and the years are spaced just as far apart as each strip needs, worked out in one longest-path pass, with the frame size chosen by binary search.
- **Music moves the pages.** The site's single audio player feeds an analyser; motion reads each song against its own loud and quiet range, so heavily compressed modern records still have beats. Only one sound plays at a time, nothing plays until you press play, and a song's progress follows the audio's own clock, so a slow-loading preview never runs ahead.
- **Animation is cheap and safe.** Movement uses only `transform` and `opacity` (or the Web Animations API), measured at 60 fps in headless Chrome. There are no flashes or white-outs anywhere, because sudden brightness changes can trigger photosensitive epilepsy, and every opening can be skipped. Reduced-motion settings are respected throughout.
- **Accessibility.** Pages are checked with axe-core; every control is reachable by keyboard, and the scenes lightbox can be walked with arrow keys.

## Running it locally

You'll need Node.js 20 or newer. No API keys are needed.

```bash
npm install
npm run build:data    # builds public/graph.json from the data (fetches photos, covers and previews)
npm run dev           # http://localhost:3000
```

| Command | What it does |
|---|---|
| `npm run dev` | Starts the dev server |
| `npm run build:data` | Rebuilds `public/graph.json` from `data/seed-data.ts` and `data/edge-sources.json` |
| `npm run build` | Production build (rebuilds the data first) |
| `npm run typecheck` | `tsc --noEmit` |
| `npm run lint` | ESLint |
| `npm test` | Runs the tests |

After rebuilding `public/graph.json`, restart the dev server: the data is cached in memory.

**Tests:** `npm test` runs 180 tests in 22 files, on every push through GitHub Actions (which also rebuilds the data, type-checks, lints and does a production build). They cover the build (a link to a missing artist fails it), the road finder, the sourcing ledger, the genre city and its labels, the day's paper, the skyline, the scenes lightbox and scene pages, the genre features, the browse bill, the family photo, the artist rooms and their instruments' string physics, music-driven motion, the player's library, the tour, today's record and the Mixtape (every song plays, and every quote matches its link's citation).
